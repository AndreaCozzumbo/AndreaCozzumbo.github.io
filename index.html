Hello there!

I’m Andrea. I’m a researcher, a cosmologist to be precise, and I’ve just finished my PhD in astroparticle physics at the Gran Sasso Science Institute.

My research is mainly about the nature of dark energy: what is making the Universe expand faster and faster? Is it really a constant, does it change with time, or is it gravity that we haven’t fully understood?

A new way to investigate what dark energy could be is with gravitational waves! I’m a member of LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA, which detects them from merging black holes and neutron stars, and of the Einstein Telescope, the detector that will hear much farther in the future.

Curious? My papers are in Publications.

And when I’m not doing physics I’m probably around with a film camera: here you can find some of my shots.

Gravitational waves give the distance \(d_L\) and light gives the redshift \(z\): what remains is \(f_{\rm DE}\).

Multi-messenger cosmology

When two neutron stars merge and we catch both the gravitational waves and the gamma-ray burst, we get a distance and a redshift at once, and I use them to find out how dark energy evolves without assuming a model for it.

Horndeski theories, the most general scalar-tensor theories with second-order equations of motion.

Modified gravity

If dark energy really crosses \(w=-1\), as recent data hint, the simplest scalar field is not enough, so I look for theories of gravity beyond Einstein’s that can do it and I test them on the data with a Boltzmann solver (CLASS).

The CMB lensing map measured by Planck (2018). ESA and the Planck Collaboration.

Neutrinos and cosmological anomalies

Cosmological data, taken at face value, seem to like neutrinos with a negative mass: I study how this odd preference is tied to an anomaly in the gravitational lensing of the cosmic microwave background.
